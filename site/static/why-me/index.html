Why me

The person specification, answered with evidence

I am an applied AI engineer. The first question I ever asked a computer was "what happens in between, and can you show me?", and it is still the question behind everything I build; your project asks it of an estimator holding a single-line diagram.

The person specification for this post lists 17 criteria: 12 essential and 5 desirable [1]. Below, each one is answered twice: with evidence from this project, which you can try on this site, and with evidence from my professional record. Where I have a gap, I say so, and say how I would close it. Figures from my earlier roles are my own measurements from the time; where I recorded how a figure was measured, the method is given.

My record in brief: an MSc in Advanced Computer Science with Research from the University of Hertfordshire; two years (August 2024 to August 2026, part-time alongside the MSc until September 2025) as a Full-Stack AI Engineer at Pro Construction Bidders, a pre-construction services firm (estimating, quantity take-offs, tender analysis and bid preparation), building the document-extraction, retrieval, agent and evaluation systems its estimators used daily; and several systems I built on my own, of which three matter here: TenderFrame (an auditable assistant that must cite its sources), GenFeedback (a fine-tuned extraction model behind a human review step) and AugmentMeReality (vision models deployed and fused on an iPhone).

Why I built this project

  • To show a partial solution, not a promise. The job description asks for drawings and specifications turned into accurate bills of materials and costed quotations, with explainability. This miniature does that end to end on synthetic drawings, with every step measured.
  • To show I can run the whole project. Data, model, rules, evaluation, deployment and documentation, planned in steps, decided on evidence and shipped to a fixed date, with the unfinished parts stated.
  • To close my largest gap before you found it. I had never trained a detector. Now I have, and the record shows how.
  • To find the hard problems early. Every problem in the log is one the KTP will meet on real drawings; each now has a work package.

My first 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 and 2: learn the work. At the estimators' desks and on the factory floor: the products, the enquiries, each step from enquiry to quotation, and where the time goes (objective 1). Agree the IT and security boundary.
  2. Weeks 2 and 3: the mini-project. Time ten recent enquiries step by step (minutes per step, rework, error types), then build a throwaway tool for the costliest step to show the size of the gain before the workplan is fixed.
  3. Weeks 3 to 6: the data. An inventory of past enquiries (drawings, specifications, bills of materials, the quotes sent), their formats and confidentiality terms; a labelling tool set up with an estimator as co-owner of the symbol set; the first sheets labelled, links included.
  4. Weeks 6 to 10: first models. The detector from this miniature retrained on company drawings behind the same interface; a first clause extractor on real specification sections; scorecards per consultant and input type.
  5. Weeks 10 to 12: the baseline. A report in the company's units: what the drawings look like by input type, the measured time per step, first model numbers on a golden set agreed with the company supervisor, and the plan for months 4 to 12, ready for the first Local Management Committee.

What I would ask the company in week one

  1. What arrives with an enquiry, in which formats (vector PDF, scan, CAD file), and how many arrive a month?
  2. Which step costs the estimators the most time today, and where do errors and rework come from?
  3. Are past enquiries kept together with the drawing, the specification, the bill of materials and the quote that was sent, and may the project use them for training and evaluation?
  4. Which estimating tools, price lists and templates are in use, who owns them, and how are price changes handled?
  5. Who owns the design rules, and are they written down?
  6. Where may models run: on the company's own servers, in a UK cloud region, or through outside services, given its security accreditation and customers' confidentiality terms?
  7. How will the company judge success: hours per quote, errors caught, win rate?
  8. Which sectors' quotations are hardest, and which estimator would co-own the symbol set?
  9. For the academic team: the supervision rhythm, access to compute, the publication plan, and whether anonymised drawings could ever be released.
