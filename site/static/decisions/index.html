Decisions

Fifteen decisions, and what each one bought

The choices that decided whether the figures on this site can be trusted, in the order I took them: the problem, the options I weighed, what I chose, and what the measurements showed afterwards. Every figure is read from the evaluation files when the page loads.

I am Muhammad Adnan, an applied AI engineer. This page is the reasoning behind sld2quote, the miniature single-line-diagram-to-quotation system on this site. It lists the fifteen decisions that mattered most, in the order I took them. Each one names the problem, the options I weighed, what I chose and what the measurements showed afterwards. Where a decision went wrong first, the page says so.

Three things hold for every number on this page:

  • The drawings are synthetic. They were drawn by the project's own generator, with invented prices. Nothing comes from R&B. Scores on synthetic drawings should be read as an upper bound for real ones.
  • The label and role rules were tuned on the sets they are scored on. The check on fresh drawings is described under decision 13.
  • Real drawings are not scored yet. Nineteen public-domain one-line diagrams are collected with their provenance; they are not labelled.

Unless a sentence says otherwise, a number is for the site's reader: the RT-DETR detector (pass 4 of the training run rtdetr_r18_plain_ema_fix), RapidOCR reading the text, and the step 6 link rules, run on 4 processor threads with no graphics card. The sets are test (30 drawings in the styles the detector trained on, none of which it saw in training), unseen style (60 drawings in a font or label layout held out of training) and baseline (the 30 drawings of the first measurement), each as a clean copy and a scan-like copy.

Each decision opens on its title; the one-line summary is always visible.

1. Train and test on synthetic drawings that carry their own answer keys

No labelled set of switchboard drawings is public, so I built a generator that draws them and writes down the right answer for each one; every stage of the pipeline is scored against it.

The problem
A detector learns from drawings in which every symbol has been marked, and every score needs a correct answer to compare with. No public labelled set of single-line diagrams exists. A 2024 review of the field names the lack of datasets as a key challenge [15], and a recent published set of 17,085 labelled symbols on single-line diagrams is confidential to the company that owns the drawings [16]. R&B's drawings could not be used before the project, and should not be.
Options considered
  • Hand-label a few public drawings: slow, few in number, in the American symbol style, and most carry no ratings.
  • Ask a large vision-language model to read the drawings with no training: there is nothing to measure it against, and published work on engineering drawings reports an 11.5% hallucination rate even for a fine-tuned model [19].
  • Write a generator that draws each sheet and its answer key together.
Decision
A generator (generate.py) draws low-voltage switchboard diagrams in varied fonts, sizes, line widths and label layouts, at two board sizes, each with a scan-like copy and a short specification that sometimes contradicts the drawing on purpose. Its answer key lists every device with its type, box and ratings, and every piece of text with its box and the device it was drawn for. Real drawings are kept for testing only, never for training.
Result
A pool of 900 drawings, split by whole drawing so that no drawing feeds both training and testing: 400 for training (9,176 tiles holding 38,332 symbol boxes), 40 for validation, 30 for the test set, 60 for the unseen-style test, and 20 more unseen-style drawings used only to choose between models. Because each drawing carries its answer key, symbols, label links, ratings, notes, conflicts, bill-of-materials lines and price are each scored on their own, not only the final total.
The technical layer
The generator is treated as a product with its own tests. An audit of early output found a label printed across a symbol on 16% of sheets, and scan-like copies whose answer-key boxes sat up to 35 pixels away from the rotated ink; both are fixed and now checked by tests (problems 3 and 4 in the log). Work on P&ID digitisation shows that synthetic sheets which keep real topology can train a model to within 8 points of one trained on real drawings [18]. That is the role synthetic data plays here: a way to start, not a substitute for the company's drawings. The 19 real drawings come from US federal documents, which are public domain [14].

HANDOFF.md section 7, step 2; docs/PROBLEMS.md entries 2 to 4; data/real/SOURCES.md.

2. Draw the international symbols, for every device an estimator prices

The drawings use IEC 60617 symbols for sixteen device types, so the detector is tested on the parts that change a price, not only on breakers.

The problem
The first generator drew three shapes: breakers, transformers and generators. An estimator's take-off also counts fuse switches, isolators, motor starters, power-factor correction, surge protection, current transformers, meters and protection relays. A detector trained on three shapes would prove nothing about the rest.
Options considered
  • Invent simple shapes: easy, but no consultant draws them.
  • Copy symbols from manufacturers' or consultants' drawings: the licence is unclear, and the work would be tied to one firm's style.
  • Use the international standard symbols from an openly licensed library, with attribution.
Decision
IEC 60617, the IEC's database of graphical symbols for electrotechnical diagrams [9]. Seven symbol bodies are taken unchanged from the QElectroTech elements collection through the synergycodes single-line-diagram editor, under CC BY 3.0 and credited [11], [12], [13]; the other shapes are drawn to the same conventions. Breakers appear in both the IEC style (a switch with a cross) and the box-with-a-diagonal style common on UK drawings. The detector learns 14 classes, one per symbol meaning (one breaker class covers incomers, outgoing breakers and bus-sections, which the role rules tell apart), and each of the 16 device types has a pricing rule.
Result
The wider set exposed the hand-written shape tests at once. On the test set (clean copies) the classical reader finds of the symbols, because it knows only box-style breakers and circles; the trained reader finds .
The technical layer
The answer key does not depend on how a symbol looks: a device's type comes from the generator, so the same key scores both breaker styles and either reader. Still missing: a changeover-switch symbol, withdrawable brackets, double-line busbars, and the American ANSI/IEEE 315 style that the real public-domain drawings use.

assets/symbols/ATTRIBUTION.md; docs/PROBLEMS.md entries 1 and 5; eval/RESULTS.md, classical and trained sections.

3. RT-DETR rather than YOLO, and the smaller model by measurement

I chose a detector whose licence lets a company build it into its own software without publishing that software, then picked its size by timing and scoring both options.

The problem
The reader needs a modern object detector that can be fine-tuned on drawings and that a manufacturer could later ship inside its own estimating tools.
Options considered
  • Ultralytics YOLO, the most popular family. It is offered under AGPL-3.0, which the vendor explains as publicly releasing the complete source of the whole derived work, or under a paid Enterprise licence for closed use [25].
  • RT-DETR, a real-time detection transformer whose code and pretrained weights are Apache-2.0, available through the Hugging Face transformers library [20], [21], [22], [23].
  • torchvision's Faster R-CNN (BSD licence), kept as the fallback.
Decision
RT-DETR, fine-tuned from the COCO-trained PekingU/rtdetr_r18vd checkpoint (about 20 million parameters) through RTDetrForObjectDetection [22], [23]. Its authors report that it beats comparable YOLO models on both speed and accuracy on the COCO benchmark [20]; I did not train YOLO, so that comparison is theirs, not mine. Between the two backbone sizes, I measured rather than assumed. The smaller r18vd read a standard sheet in about half the time of r50vd on every device tried (0.82 s against 1.65 s on 8 processor threads, 1.31 s against 2.45 s on 2). Scored per tile on the 60-drawing unseen-style test after the first overnight runs, it was also the more accurate (mAP@0.5 0.922 against 0.887), while r50vd found about one relay in seven.
Result
The site's model scores mAP@0.5 per tile on the unseen-style test. On whole drawings of the test set (clean copies), the share of symbols found rises from with the classical reader to , and the share of reported boxes that are right from to .
The technical layer
RT-DETR is end to end. Like DETR, it is trained with one-to-one matching between its predictions and the true objects, which teaches it to avoid duplicate boxes without a hand-written suppression step [24], [20]. That behaviour takes a long training schedule to learn, which shaped decision 4. Licence review is part of model selection from the first day: the symbol artwork is CC BY 3.0 and credited, and the text reader's OpenCV wheel bundles LGPL components (problem 18 in the log).
Evidence
HANDOFF.md section 7, step 3 (the speed bench and the overnight result); eval/DETECTOR.md; docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 18.

4. Overlapping tiles, and one rule against counting a part twice

A drawing is read in overlapping squares, and a filter makes sure that no symbol becomes two lines on the quote.

The problem
Sheets are 1,600 to about 4,300 pixels wide and the detector reads 640-pixel squares; shrinking a page to fit would make small symbols vanish. Cutting the page creates a new problem: symbols cut in half at a tile edge. And the first trained model drew two boxes on one symbol: 92% of its false alarms were duplicates of a symbol it had already found. On a quotation, a duplicate is a part priced twice.
Options considered
  • Shrink the whole page to one image: small symbols are lost.
  • Tiles with no overlap: every symbol on a seam is cut.
  • Overlapping tiles, merged afterwards, with duplicates removed.
  • Wait for the detector to learn to suppress its own duplicates, which DETR-style models do over a long schedule: the authors' recipe runs 72 passes over the data [21], and the first run had 2.
Decision
640-pixel tiles with a 160-pixel overlap (a stride of 480), the last tile flush with the page edge, so that every symbol is whole in at least one tile. Inside each tile, duplicate suppression across classes (non-maximum suppression at IoU 0.5) is part of the detector's single inference path. Across tiles, two merge filters: a box cut by an inner tile edge is dropped, and a box that mostly contains a better-scoring one is dropped. Reading large images in slices is established practice for small objects [26].
Result
On the first run, duplicate suppression alone lifted the validation mAP@0.5 from 0.854 to 0.933. For the current run, on the unseen-style test tiles, the detector reports false alarms with suppression and without. The two merge filters added 2 to 6 points of precision on whole drawings for at most half a point of recall. A plausible refinement, keeping a cut box when no whole one exists elsewhere, was measured and rejected: it added 6 to 15 false boxes per set to regain about 3 true ones.
The technical layer
Suppression across classes matters: if one symbol is seen as two different classes, suppression within each class would keep both and the quote would carry two different parts. Per-tile scores are a floor. In its own tile, a symbol cut by the edge is found of the time, yet on whole unseen-style drawings (clean copies) of symbols are found, because each one is whole in a neighbouring tile.

sld2quote/tiles.py; eval/DETECTOR.md; docs/PROBLEMS.md entries 6 and 11; HANDOFF.md section 7, steps 3 and 4.

5. Set the detector's cut-off on whole drawings, not on tiles

The score above which a box counts as a symbol is chosen on whole validation drawings, because that is the unit an estimator sees, and blank paper is where invented parts appear.

The problem
A control model's cut-off, chosen to maximise accuracy on validation tiles, came out at 0.06. On whole drawings it let through 35 to 45 "capacitors" per test set, scoring 0.06 to 0.08 and sitting on blank paper with nothing under them, some over 100 pixels wide. The price barely moved only because capacitor ratings were not read yet; once they were, every ghost would have become a priced power-factor-correction line.
Options considered
  • The per-tile cut-off that maximises tile accuracy.
  • A separate cut-off for each class.
  • One cut-off chosen by sweeping whole validation drawings through the detector and the tile merge, and counting the boxes that land on blank paper.
Decision
The third. The sweep runs over whole validation sheets (the clean and scan-like copies of the seen-style and unseen-style validation drawings), and the chosen value is stored with the weights, apart from the per-tile one. Test drawings are never used for choosing. Boxes on blank paper (a false box with almost no ink inside it) are reported as their own number, because each one is a line on a quote.
Result
For the site's model the sweep chose . At that value the validation drawings carry boxes on blank paper, the share of reported boxes that are right is and the share of symbols found is . At 0.06 the same drawings would carry boxes on blank paper.
The technical layer
Tiles that hold symbols dominate a tile score, so a low cut-off costs little there. A whole drawing is mostly blank paper, where a low cut-off has far more room to invent things. The two measurements disagree, and the product lives at the drawing level.

sld2quote/select_threshold.py; eval/charts.json (threshold); docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 10b.

6. Choose the best training pass by per-class F1 at the working cut-off, not by mAP

When choosing which saved pass of a model to keep, I score it the way it will be used, class by class at its working cut-off, so that one class failing cannot hide inside an average.

The problem
The usual detection score, mAP, averages over classes and over every possible cut-off, so one class failing at the cut-off actually used barely moves it. Three gentle extra passes on the first full model lifted the validation mAP@0.5 from 0.929 to 0.936, while on the unseen style the share of generators found fell from 0.98 to 0.50 per tile.
Options considered
  • Validation mAP@0.5.
  • mAP averaged over the seen-style and unseen-style validation sets.
  • The worst class's lower value of precision and recall.
  • Mean per-class F1 at the working cut-off, pooled over both validation sets.
Decision
Pooled mean per-class F1 at the working cut-off, over the seen-style and unseen-style validation drawings. Every pass's weights are kept with all the candidate scores recorded, so any other rule can be applied later without retraining.
Result
In the comparisons of 18 October, F1 picked the model that the untouched test set then confirmed. mAP would have picked the continuation run, which generalised worse (per tile on the 60-drawing unseen-style test, 0.918 against 0.922 for the model it continued). For the current run, the rule picked pass 10, with pooled F1 ; decision 9 explains why the site still uses pass 4.
The technical layer
F1 at the working cut-off is closer to the bar the project set itself, 95% precision and 95% recall for every class, than an average over cut-offs that are never used. mAP@0.5 is still reported, computed with pycocotools [28], so the numbers can be compared with published work.
Evidence
eval/DETECTOR.md (training curves); docs/PROBLEMS.md entries 8 and 9.

7. Hold some drawing styles out of training altogether

Drawings in a font and a label layout that the model never trained on are kept apart, so the scores show how it copes with a consultant whose drawings it has never seen.

The problem
Drafting style varies from consultant to consultant. A model can score well on the styles it trained on and fail on the next firm's drawings, and a selection step that only sees trained styles will quietly prefer the model that generalises worst.
Options considered
  • A random split of drawings: every style appears in training, so nothing measures generalisation.
  • Hold out a font, a label layout, or both.
  • Use the held-out drawings for choosing as well as for testing: then the test is no longer independent.
Decision
The unseen style holds out the Courier New font and the labels-below layout. There are two separate held-out sets: 20 drawings for choosing between models and 60 for the final numbers only. The final set was widened from 20 to 60 drawings, because with 24 relay boxes a 95% recall bar allowed a single miss.
Result
The held-out style is where most of the remaining error lives: ratings are read right of the time on unseen-style clean copies against on the test set, most of the gap from one cause (a cable drawn through its own label, problem 27 in the log). The held-out set also corrected an explanation. The first one blamed the held-out font for the loss of symbols with a letter inside, such as the G of a generator. Checking the generator showed that those letters were drawn in Arial Bold on every sheet, so the font could not change them; the labels-below layout, used on most unseen-style drawings, was the likelier cause.
The technical layer
The unseen style is still this generator's style: the same symbols and the same drafting conventions. A new consultant's drawings will differ in more ways than a font and a layout, so in the real project the held-out unit should be whole consultants.
Evidence
sld2quote/export_coco.py; eval/RESULTS.md, unseen sections; docs/PROBLEMS.md entries 8 and 15.

8. Average the weights, as the detector's authors do

Instead of keeping the model exactly as it stands at the end of a pass, I keep a running average of its weights, which smooths out lucky and unlucky moments in training.

The problem
Across the ten saved passes of one run, the share of a device type found swung by up to 0.3 between neighbouring passes, even late in training (motors 0.84, then 0.60). So "which pass" was deciding the per-type results, and comparisons between runs were partly comparisons between lucky and unlucky passes.
Options considered
  • More passes, or a lower learning rate.
  • Choose passes by a smoother score.
  • Follow the authors' own recipe. RT-DETR's official configuration keeps an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights, with decay 0.9999 and a 2,000-update warm-up, and evaluates the average rather than the live weights [21]. The Hugging Face port provides the model but not that part of the recipe.
Decision
EMA added to the trainer as one separately measured change, with a control run on the same data without it. The decay is scaled to this run's length: 0.999 with 200 warm-up updates averages over about 1,000 steps. The authors' setting, tuned to 72 passes, would still be warming up at the end of these runs and would average over only about 18 steps.
Result
Per tile on the unseen-style test, the control run scores mAP@0.5 and the same recipe with EMA , the first model to beat the earlier product model (). Its validation curve rose steadily instead of see-sawing. The lesson: read the authors' configuration files, not only the paper; a port of a model is not a port of its training.
Evidence
eval/DETECTOR.md, sections rtdetr_r18_plain, rtdetr_r18_plain_ema and rtdetr_r18_full; docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 10a.

9. Pass 4, not pass 10: judged on whole drawings

The last training run's final pass scored slightly higher on tiles, but an earlier pass made fewer mistakes on whole drawings, so the site uses the earlier one.

The problem
After a fault in the Mac's graphics chip was found and worked around (problem 17a in the log), the best recipe was run again. Scored per tile, the selection rule of decision 6 preferred the final pass, pass 10 (pooled F1 against for pass 4). But the product is judged on drawings, and decisions 4 and 5 had already shown that tile scores and drawing scores can disagree.
Options considered
  • Ship the pass that the tile rule chose.
  • Put the leading candidates through the whole-drawing sweep of decision 5, each with its own cut-off, and compare them on the validation drawings.
Decision
The second. Pass 4 became the site's model, stored with its own cut-off, its confidence calibration and a note of where it came from.
Result
On whole validation drawings, pass 4 reached a mean class F1 of with boxes on blank paper, against 0.950 and 33 for pass 10. Per tile the two are close: mAP@0.5 against on the unseen-style test.
The technical layer
Every pass's weights had been kept (decision 6), so this check cost an hour of evaluation, not a new training run.

10. Show a calibrated confidence, not the detector's raw score

The confidence on each box now means "how often a box like this was right on the validation drawings", which an estimator can act on.

The problem
The results page painted almost every device red, "low confidence", on drawings where every symbol had been found. The number shown was the detector's raw score, and the colour bands had been set for the old shape-matching reader, whose scores behaved like percentages. A DETR's scores rank boxes; they are not probabilities, and modern neural networks are known to be poorly calibrated [29].
Options considered
  • Keep the raw score and move the colour bands by hand.
  • Rescale the scores with one fitted curve, such as the single temperature that Guo and colleagues found effective [29]; this assumes one smooth curve can correct every band of score.
  • A table from bands of score to measured precision, built on validation drawings with the same sweep that sets the cut-off.
Decision
The table. Each model's weights carry it: for each band of score, how often such a box was right on the validation drawings. The raw score is kept in the result for the record; the page says what the number means and that it was calibrated on synthetic drawings. A device's colour is now decided mostly by whether its rating label was read, which is what an estimator needs to know.
Result
For the site's model, a box with a raw score from 0.11 to 0.16 is shown with a confidence of , because boxes in that band were right that often on the validation drawings; a box scoring from 0.24 to 0.36 is shown with .
The technical layer
A table measured on whole drawings needs no assumption about the shape of the score distribution, and it is rebuilt by the same command whenever the model changes. Its limit is stated on the page: on real drawings it overstates, because they are not the drawings it was measured on.
Evidence
eval/charts.json (calibration); docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 22.

11. Choose the Linux text reader by measuring it on our own drawings

The site runs on Linux, where Apple's text reader does not exist, so I measured four open engines (one of them with two recognisers) on the project's drawings and took the most accurate one that is fast enough.

The problem
The first text reader was Apple Vision, which runs only on a Mac. A public site runs on Linux.
Options considered
Tesseract, EasyOCR, docTR, and RapidOCR, which runs PaddleOCR's PP-OCR models on ONNX Runtime [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], with its bundled Chinese recogniser or the English one; Apple Vision as the reference.
Decision
A rule written before the measurement: the best character accuracy on the rating labels and the notes lines (a string the engine misses scores 0), among engines that read a standard sheet in under about 5 seconds on 2 processor threads; if two are within 0.01, the lighter install wins.
Result
RapidOCR with the English recogniser: in seconds per standard sheet, against for Apple Vision, for Tesseract, for docTR, and for EasyOCR, which took seconds and broke the time rule. On the held-out font, Courier New, RapidOCR reads labels at against Vision's .
The technical layer
The bundled Chinese recogniser drops the spaces in English labels ("ACB-13200A4P"), hence the English one. With RapidOCR, the close-up second pass around each symbol reads labels worse than its own page pass ( against ), because strokes inside the window are read as text, and it costs seconds per sheet, so the site turns it off. The engine sits behind one function, so another can be swapped in and scored the same way.
Evidence
eval/OCR.md; docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 13.

12. Measure the link between a symbol and its label on its own

Reading a label correctly is no use if it is attached to the wrong device, so the link got its own score, and then a new rule.

The problem
On a 10-drawing probe, 26 of 42 wrong ratings had the label read exactly but attached to the wrong device. The scorecard could not tell a wrong link from a missed symbol or a misread label, because the answer key did not record which device each text was drawn for.
Options considered
  • Keep the rule "nearest text to the right or below".
  • Train a second model to make the link: there were no labelled links to train it on.
  • Rules from the drawing's own structure, scored on their own.
Decision
The generator now records which device each text belongs to; the 240 answer keys of the three evaluation sets were refreshed without changing a pixel of any drawing, which was checked. The scorecard gains link precision and link recall. The rule, agreed on 17 October: symbols on one vertical line form a way; text lines stacked at regular spacing form the way's label block; the block goes to the way with the shortest edge-to-edge gap; each line goes to the device it names (a current rating to the breaker, kW to the motor starter, kVAr to the capacitor, kVA and kV to the source, a CT ratio to the current transformer, a cable size to the way; text inside a symbol is its tag; the rest is the destination).
Result
On the test set (clean copies), link precision rose from to and link recall from to ; ratings read right from to ; mean price error from to . On unseen-style clean copies, ratings read right rose from to and mean price error fell from to .
The technical layer
These rules were designed by reading the per-link error lists of the same sets they are scored on, so those numbers are in sample, and the results file says so in its header. The check on drawings no rule was read from is decision 13.

13. Have the riskiest logic reviewed independently, on fresh drawings

The link and role rules were the riskiest hand-written logic in the project, so they were reviewed by a reviewer that had not written them, and tested on fresh drawings nobody had looked at.

The problem
Rules designed by reading error lists on the scored sets can fit those sets and fail elsewhere, and whoever builds a rule is the person least likely to see its blind spots.
Options considered
  • Trust the scores.
  • Generate more scored sets and keep tuning.
  • An independent review, with checks on drawings that no rule was read from.
Decision
A separate review agent, which had not built step 6, checked that no answer-key information leaked into the reader, verified the 240 refreshed answer keys byte for byte, and ran answer-key oracles: the generator's own symbols, texts and busbars fed to the rules on fresh drawings, scored by the same link score and rating checks.
Result
The review found one real bug. On 10 of 1,530 classic drawings, a long first label line ended 19 to 27 pixels from the next way's symbol at the same height, the block went to the neighbour, and 22 ratings were wrong; the side preference is now a ranking, not a small penalty. The window below a symbol was also widened for layouts whose labels start under the cable. On 500 fresh drawings of each font set, used for no choice, the reviewed rules make no link or rating error, where the first version misrated 12 devices (classic fonts) and 233 (wide fonts). The in-sample tuning is stated plainly in the results header and in the problem log.
Evidence
HANDOFF.md section 7, 19 October 15:10; eval/RESULTS.md header; docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 24.

14. Deploy inside the free tier, with no secret on the server

The live site costs nothing while nobody visits, and the server holds no password or token that could leak.

The problem
The first deployment fetched the model from a private repository using the owner's write token, far more power than a server that reads one file should hold. It also kept the processor on so that the models could load in a background thread, which would have cost about $110 a month if anything kept the service awake around the clock.
Options considered
  • Keep the processor allocated all the time (instance-based billing) [38].
  • Replace the write token with a read-only token for the one model.
  • Copy the model into the container image and need no token at all.
  • Load the models inside the first request that needs them, so request-based billing works.
Decision
Request-based billing, under which an instance is charged only while it starts, serves requests and shuts down, and has processor time only while it serves [38]. The models load inside the first request instead of a background thread, and a TCP startup probe lets the page answer quickly after a cold start [39]. The model is copied into the image and the token was destroyed; the service runs under its own account. A daily cap on live runs keeps the worst case inside the free allowance, which for request-based billing is 180,000 vCPU-seconds, 360,000 GB-seconds and 2 million requests a month [42]. The second-generation execution environment is used, which Google documents as having faster CPU performance [41]; on the first deployment it halved a run that took 95 seconds in the default sandbox.
Result
Measured on the live service on 19 October: the page answers in about 3 seconds from cold; the models load within the first run (13 to 22 seconds); a streamed live run of a medium generated drawing took 34 to 54 seconds (60 seconds on one slow host), and an upload 12 to 19 seconds. The service has no secret to steal.
Evidence
scripts/deploy_cloudrun.sh; docs/PROBLEMS.md entry 21; HANDOFF.md section 7, 19 October 01:15 to 03:15.

15. One link for everything

Everything you need is behind one link: try the system live, see how it works, and read the evidence, the decisions, the problems and the plan.

The problem
An assessor or a company director will click one link and give it a few minutes. A video or a static page proves nothing about whether the system runs, and a code repository asks too much of a reader who is not a developer.
Options considered
A replayed static site; GitHub Pages; a shared document page; a Hugging Face Space, where the free plan refused a Docker Space (payment required, HTTP 402); Google Cloud Run.
Decision
A live site on Cloud Run. It runs the real pipeline on a stored sample, a freshly generated drawing or an upload, streams each stage as it happens with what that stage produced, scores generated drawings against their answer keys while you watch, and serves its scorecards from the same evaluation files as these pages. Every sample also has a precomputed result, so nothing depends on the server waking up.
Result
Live runs on demand; a second visitor is served while a run is in progress (problem 21 in the log); and the numbers on these pages are read from the evaluation files, so they cannot drift from the record.
Evidence
site/app.py; HANDOFF.md section 2 ("One-link site") and section 7.

What the fifteen have in common

  • Measure on the unit the product is judged by. Whole drawings, not tiles; price and bill-of-materials lines, not only boxes.
  • Never let a model be the authority on a price. Rules price; doubtful items are raised for the estimator, never silently fixed; every line names its evidence.
  • Write the problem down when it happens. Every decision above came from a measurement, and several from a mistake I recorded first (the problem log).
